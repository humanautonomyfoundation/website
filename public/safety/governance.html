AI Governance Map

Who sets the rules for AI, how binding those rules are, and what they actually cover. The EU, the US, China, the UK and international bodies are taking very different approaches, and decisions about the most dangerous models still rest mainly with companies.

The short version

Six things to know

The EU has the only comprehensive AI law

The AI Act's rules for big model makers have been enforceable since August 2026. Its rules for high-risk uses were pushed back to late 2027 and 2028.

The US has no comprehensive federal AI law

Narrower laws exist, such as the TAKE IT DOWN Act on deepfake intimate images. Federal policy runs mainly on executive orders that favor speed and national security and seek to override state rules.

US states fill the gap

States enacted 109 AI laws in 29 states by 1 July 2026, down from 121 in 39 states a year earlier (NYU via Tech Policy Press). California has required frontier developers to publish safety frameworks and report incidents since January 2026. New York's similar law starts in January 2027; Illinois enacted one in July 2026.

China regulates content and services

Binding rules cover labeling, companion apps and pre-launch filing. A comprehensive national AI law is now on the agenda.

International efforts are non-binding

Summits, a UN science panel and dialogue, and a new US–China incident channel. The one treaty is not yet in force.

Companies still decide when to pause

Binding transparency and risk-management duties now apply in the EU and California (Lawfare). But pausing a model is still mostly left to company frameworks, which often use loose “may consider” wording (SaferAI), apart from one-off government actions.

    Expert views

    What the research says

    What analysts and researchers say about the rules on this page, including where they disagree with it or qualify it.

    • Supporters of one national standard, such as TechFreedom’s Andy Jung, say only Congress can replace the 50-state patchwork, and that gridlock is the main obstacle (WLF Legal Pulse, Jun 2026).
    • Opponents say preemption would set a federal ceiling before AI harms are understood, and that even the narrower FRONTIER Act would stop states going further on frontier transparency, audits and incident reporting (The Regulatory Review, Oct 2026).
    • Brookings analysts call California’s SB 53 “governance by transparency” that turns voluntary pledges into enforceable duties, but warn that its reports could become boilerplate and that its compute threshold creates a “regulatory cliff” (Brookings, Dec 2025).
    • Industry groups such as NetChoice say frontier-safety laws wrongly make developers answer for misuse they cannot foresee, instead of the bad actors who cause it (NetChoice, 2025).
    • Independent raters find lab safety frameworks weak as accountability tools, which qualifies the companies’ own descriptions in the industry section above: SaferAI scored them between 8% and 34% against risk-management standards, and FLI gave no company better than a C+ (SaferAI, 2026; FLI, Jul 2026).
    • Supporters of the September 2026 White House industry accord say it is more realistic than an international treaty, while critics note that the companies wrote the principles and hire their own auditors (Al Jazeera, Sep 2026).
    • Export-control analysts warn that the 2026 rule allowing some advanced chip sales to China relies on conditions the US cannot verify, and Epoch estimates that hundreds of thousands of top-end-equivalent chips were smuggled into China by the end of 2025, a highly uncertain figure that both sides of the debate cite (IAPS, Jan 2026; Epoch AI, Apr 2026).
    • On open-weight models, a group of about 25 firms led by Nvidia, Microsoft and Meta warns against “premature restrictions”, Anthropic opposes blanket bans but backs safety testing for the most powerful models, and the US administration has threatened sanctions on Chinese open-model labs (Tom’s Hardware, Jul 2026; SiliconANGLE, Jul 2026).
    • The EU’s AI Office now has sweeping legal powers over frontier models but may be understaffed, and digital-rights groups call the 2026 delay of high-risk rules deregulation, while the Commission says it means “less paperwork” without weaker goals (Lawfare, May 2026; EDRi, Jun 2026; Didomi).
    • Researchers say an international AI agreement could be checked today with on-site inspections, but tamper-proof chip tracking and secure model testing still need research, and 20 countries and the EU want any new body to “enable verification” (MIRI, Mar 2026; Al Jazeera, Sep 2026).
