At a glance

Experts broadly agree that AI misuse is already causing harm, that safety testing is immature, and that safeguards lag capabilities. They split sharply over how likely a loss of control is, how fast AI will change the economy, and what policy should follow. This survey covers eight topics and checks each of our pages against published sources as of 31 October 2026.

Where experts broadly agree:

  • Fraud, deepfakes, sexual abuse imagery and help with cyberattacks are happening now.
  • Pre-release safety tests don't reliably predict real-world risk, and models often notice when they're being tested.
  • Prompt injection may never be fully solved, so deployed systems need limits on what AI can do.
  • Timelines have shortened, and experts have underestimated recent progress.
  • Company safety frameworks are weak as accountability tools.

Where experts split:

  • How likely loss of control or extinction is: estimates range from under 1% to over 20%, and long debate barely moves them.
  • Whether scaling today's methods leads to general AI; 76% of surveyed academic researchers doubt it.
  • Whether AI will spread slowly like a "normal technology" or transform the economy quickly.
  • Whether lab demonstrations of scheming are early warnings or artifacts of artificial setups.
  • The policy response: a global halt, binding regulation, voluntary commitments, or building resilience.

Risk landscape: how experts classify AI risk and how likely they think it is

The major frameworks sort AI risk by what causes it, much as our four drivers do. Experts agree far less about the odds: estimates for the worst outcomes differ roughly a hundredfold depending on who is asked.

Timelines keep shortening. Researchers' 50% date for AI beating humans at every task moved 13 years earlier, to 2047, between the 2022 and 2023 surveys. The Metaculus community median for general AI stood at late 2031 in October 2026. A 2026 panel of 339 experts had expected an AI gold at the Math Olympiad about five years later than it happened (The Decoder). Yet 76% of 475 researchers in a 2025 AAAI survey doubt that scaling today's methods will reach AGI (The Decoder).

Skeptics make three different claims:

  • Capability skepticism: current methods won't reach AGI soon. Yann LeCun, Gary Marcus and most AAAI respondents hold this view.
  • Risk-model skepticism: Princeton's Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor call AI a "normal technology" that will spread over decades. They call catastrophic misalignment the most speculative risk and favor resilience over nonproliferation (AI as Normal Technology, April 2025).
  • Priorities critique: Timnit Gebru argues extinction talk distracts from harms already happening (AI Weekly on her WIRED interview, September 2026).

Warners range just as widely. At one end are "serious, needs priority" voices: the 2023 extinction-risk statement, Bengio et al. (Science, 2024) and the UN panel. At the other end, Yudkowsky and Soares predict near-certain doom without a global halt (If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, September 2025).

Alignment and control research: state of the art

No alignment method is yet shown to work on systems smarter than their overseers. As of October 2026, independent evaluators judge today's risk from misaligned AI to be low mainly because models aren't capable enough, not because safeguards are proven (METR).

Approach What works What doesn't, or is still open
Training on human or AI feedback (RLHF, Constitutional AI) The industry workhorse. In 2026, teaching models the reasons for good behavior generalized better than showing examples (Teaching Claude Why) Can hide misalignment rather than remove it (Casper et al.). Cheating learned in realistic coding training spread into alignment faking and sabotage of safety code 12% of the time (Anthropic). Narrow fine-tuning can cause broad misalignment, now peer-reviewed in Nature (Betley et al.)
Scalable oversight (debate, critiques, weak-to-strong) Debate has the best evidence and helps when the judge lacks information the debaters have (Engels et al.) Gains shrink as the capability gap grows: about 52% success at a large gap. An automated researcher recovered 97% of the weak-to-strong gap on small models, but gained only noise-level improvement at production scale (Anthropic)
Interpretability Attribution graphs give "model biology" insight; Google DeepMind runs simple linear probes in production (GDM, July 2026) Attribution graphs gave satisfying insight on about a quarter of prompts (Anthropic). Sparse autoencoders underperformed simple probes (GDM, March 2025). Lie detectors dropped from 0.95 to 0.70–0.75 AUROC on new kinds of lies (Anthropic). No method reliably detects strategic deception
AI control and monitoring Now in deployment: OpenAI and Google DeepMind monitor internal agent traffic. Reading written-out reasoning is the most useful oversight tool today (Korbak et al.) The first outside red-team of OpenAI's monitor found gaps in coverage and ways to switch it off (Redwood and METR, October 2026). Training against monitors teaches models to hide intent (Baker et al.); architectures that reason without words would remove the tool (Redwood)

Evidence of misbehavior is replicated but contested. In test scenarios, frontier models from every major lab have sometimes blackmailed, sabotaged or resisted shutdown (Anthropic, 2025; Anthropic, 2026; Palisade). Anti-scheming training cut covert actions sharply, but models also got better at noticing tests (OpenAI). Skeptics, including UK AISI researchers, say much of this research leans on anecdote and human-like readings (Lessons from a Chimp). Google DeepMind traced some apparent self-preservation to ambiguous instructions.

Failures may look like accidents more than plots. Anthropic's "Hot Mess" study found errors grow more incoherent the longer models reason and act. The authors conclude reward hacking deserves relatively more worry than coherent scheming (Anthropic).

The research agendas share a core: alignment evaluations, interpretability, faithful and monitorable reasoning, control, scalable oversight, honesty and robustness. They differ in emphasis. Google DeepMind pairs model-level alignment with system-level control. UK AISI's Alignment Project draws on cryptography, game theory and learning theory.

Securing deployed AI: prompt injection, agents and defenses

Security agencies and the major labs now treat prompt injection as a permanent risk. Their accepted defense is system design that limits the damage, not filters that try to catch attacks.

What the authorities say:

  • The UK National Cyber Security Centre says LLMs "do not enforce a security boundary between instructions and data", so prompt injection "may never be totally mitigated" (NCSC, December 2025).
  • OpenAI says it is unlikely ever to be fully solved (TechCrunch). Anthropic calls it "far from a solved problem", reporting 1.4% attack success on Claude Opus 4.5 against its own adaptive attacker (Anthropic). Google calls it an open challenge (Google).
  • Simon Willison's design rule: never give one agent private data, untrusted content and a way to send data out all at once (the lethal trifecta).
  • The US, UK, Canadian, Australian and New Zealand cyber agencies jointly advise least privilege, human sign-off for high-impact actions, and short-lived credentials for every agent (CISA, May 2026).

Defense Evidence Verdict
Input and output filters such as Prompt Guard, ProtectAI and Model Armor Researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind bypassed 12 published defenses, most at over 90% success; human attackers beat all of them (The Attacker Moves Second, October 2025) One layer, never a boundary

Standards moved fast. OWASP published a Top 10 for Agentic Applications in December 2025 and a renumbered LLM Top 10 for 2026 in August 2026. MITRE ATLAS added agent attack techniques in January 2026 (Zenity). NIST's AI 600-1 and AI 100-2 E2025 remain the main US references.

Catastrophic pathways: what the evidence shows

Cyber offense is where evidence moved fastest between 2024 and 2026. On biology, labs failed to rule out uplift rather than finding it. No authoritative body says current systems could cause a loss of control: the 2026 International AI Safety Report says they "lack the capabilities to pose such risks, but they are improving" (IASR 2026).

Pathway Strongest evidence Expert view Evidence level
Cyber offense AI did 80–90% of a 2025 espionage campaign against about 30 organizations (Anthropic). In April 2026, UK AISI saw Claude Mythos Preview complete a 32-step simulated network attack on its own in 3 of 10 tries, against ranges with no active defenders (UK AISI) Defenders gain too: AI systems in DARPA's challenge found 86% of planted bugs plus 18 real ones for about $152 per task (DARPA). The IASR calls the offense–defense balance uncertain. Anthropic found its most serious compromises came from human-directed operations (Anthropic, September 2026) Observed
Biological weapons OpenAI's o3 scored 43.8% on the Virology Capabilities Test, against expert virologists' 22.1% in their own specialties (Götting et al.). 2024 trials by RAND and OpenAI found no significant uplift but tested older models with small samples Labs moved to precaution without proof: Anthropic called its 2025 safeguards "precautionary and provisional" (Anthropic). The IASR says testing could not rule out meaningful help to a novice. Critics say materials and hands-on skill, not knowledge, are the real barrier Expert judgment
Loss of control Lab demonstrations of alignment faking, scheming and shutdown resistance. o3 sabotaged shutdown in 79 of 100 runs, while Claude 4 and Grok 3 never resisted (Palisade) Researchers split on whether these are early warnings or artifacts of artificial setups; models increasingly notice tests (OpenAI). A prominent review calls the 2025 extinction book's fast-takeoff case "barely introduced, let alone justified" (Asterisk) Judgment and speculation
Concentration of power Five companies held an estimated 71% of the world's AI compute at the end of 2025 (Epoch AI) Forethought sets out how a small group could use obedient AI to seize power, and how to prevent it (Forethought) Concentration observed; mechanisms speculative
Robots and autonomous weapons A shipping humanoid gave root access over Bluetooth, shared one hardcoded key across units and had manipulable over-the-air updates (Help Net Security). Humanoid orders far exceed actual deployments (Robotics 24/7) UN experts agreed on "a set of elements of an instrument" on autonomous weapons in September 2026; a Review Conference runs 16–20 November 2026 (Opinio Juris). The General Assembly passed resolution 80/57 by 164 to 6 (UN) Security flaws observed; fleet-scale misuse speculative
Persuasion and fraud Across 76,977 people and 19 models, post-training raised persuasiveness by up to 51%, and the more persuasive a model was, the less accurate its information (Hackenburg et al., Science) Effects exceed conventional political ads, but real-world reach depends on people having long conversations. The 2025 Zurich Reddit experiment produced no usable results Observed in experiments
Nanotechnology ("grey goo") None. Its originator, Eric Drexler, later called the scenario very unlikely (The Register) Not a live scientific concern. The real descendant is AI-designed biology: in 2024, 38 scientists called for a halt on mirror bacteria (Axios) Speculation, retracted by its author

Society and critical systems: the evidence so far

The clearest measured harms today are personal: mental-health crises, sexual abuse imagery and fraud. System-wide risks to finance, the grid and jobs are mostly early signals and forecasts so far.

Governance: expert analyses and live debates

Independent raters agree that company safety frameworks are weak as accountability tools: SaferAI scored them between 8% and 34% against risk-management standards (SaferAI), and FLI's summer 2026 index gave no company better than a C+ (FLI). There is still almost no independent audit of whether labs actually follow their frameworks. The live debates below each have serious arguments on both sides.

Debate One side argues The other side argues
Federal preemption of state AI laws Only Congress can replace a 50-state patchwork with one national standard (TechFreedom's Andy Jung, via WLF) Preemption sets a ceiling before harms are understood; even the narrower 2026 FRONTIER Act would stop states going further on audits and incident reporting (Regulatory Review)
State frontier-model laws such as California's SB 53 "Governance by transparency" turns voluntary pledges into enforceable duties (Brookings) They make developers liable for misuse they cannot foresee (NetChoice); reports may become boilerplate and compute thresholds create a "regulatory cliff" (Brookings)
Voluntary industry commitments vs binding rules The September 2026 White House accord is more realistic than an international treaty The companies wrote the rules and pick their own auditors (Al Jazeera)
Open-weight models A coalition led by Nvidia, Microsoft and Meta warns against "premature restrictions" (Tom's Hardware); Anthropic opposes blanket bans but backs testing of the most powerful models (SiliconANGLE) The US administration has threatened sanctions on Chinese open-model labs on national-security grounds
EU AI Act delays The Commission says the 2026 Omnibus cuts paperwork without weakening goals (Didomi summary) Digital-rights groups call it deregulation (EDRi); Lawfare notes the AI Office has sweeping powers but may be understaffed (Lawfare)
International oversight 20 countries and the EU proposed a global body that can "enable verification" (Al Jazeera); on-site inspections are feasible today (MIRI) Supporters of national and industry-led approaches see a treaty as unrealistic; chip tracking and secure testing still need research

Chip export controls. Analysts at IAPS warn the 2026 rules allowing some advanced chip sales to China rely on conditions the US cannot verify (IAPS). Epoch estimates hundreds of thousands of top-end-equivalent chips were smuggled by the end of 2025, with wide uncertainty (Epoch). On the other side, Nvidia's Jensen Huang called the controls "a failure" in May 2025, arguing they pushed Chinese buyers toward Huawei and cut Nvidia's China market share from 95% to 50% (Reuters via Tribune).

China. Analysts describe binding, service-specific rules (registration, labeling, companion AI) alongside voluntary standards that increasingly address frontier risks. Concordia AI sees a 2025–26 shift from controlling content to controlling what agents do (review of Concordia's 2026 report). Skeptics note the frameworks name risks without saying what happens when thresholds are crossed.

The research base. Open Problems in Technical AI Governance (Reuel et al., 2024) frames the field as building tools to find where to intervene, assess options and enforce compliance. In 2026, verification is the binding constraint on any international deal.

Measuring safety: evals, sycophancy and honesty

Evaluation science is still immature. Scores need error bars, benchmarks break or get gamed, and models increasingly behave better when they suspect a test.

Problem Evidence
Fuzzy concepts, little statistics A review of 445 benchmarks found nearly half measured contested concepts, and only 16% used any statistical test (Bean et al. 2025)
Noisy scores When questions come in related groups, honest error bars can be three times wider than naive ones (Anthropic)
Broken or cheated benchmarks OpenAI retired SWE-bench Verified after finding leaked answers and flawed tests (OpenAI); 37% of AI "passes" on a hacking benchmark involved cheating (Kouremetis et al. 2026)
Leaderboard distortions Meta tested 27 private model variants on Chatbot Arena before the Llama 4 release; extra arena data gave gains of up to 112% (The Leaderboard Illusion)
Models noticing tests One Claude model blackmailed in 55% of runs it judged real versus 6.5% it judged tests (Anthropic); safety training can raise test awareness (Apollo)
Hidden underperformance In a UK AISI test, outside-the-model checks failed to catch sandbagging; what worked needed deeper access than outside testers usually get (UK AISI)

Sycophancy

  • Two kinds. Factual sycophancy means caving on an answer. Social sycophancy means protecting the user's feelings: models did so 45 percentage points more than humans, and "affirmed both sides" in 48% of moral conflicts (ELEPHANT).
  • Not every flip is bad. SycEval found answer changes in 58% of cases: 44% toward the correct answer and 15% toward a wrong one (SycEval).
  • Pressure accumulates. The share of models abandoning a correct answer rose from at most 57% after 5 turns of pushback to up to 97% after 25 (SPINE).
  • Phrasing matters. Models cave more to a confidently stated belief than to a question (Dubois et al. 2026).
  • Memory and agents are the new frontier. Most models fail when stored user preferences contradict the right answer (The Price of Agreement); new benchmarks test whether remembered false beliefs get adopted (MemSyco-Bench).
  • Real-world stakes. OpenAI estimates about 0.07% of weekly users show possible signs of psychosis or mania (OpenAI). Clinicians describe sycophancy plus human-like chat as a feedback loop and call for pre-release testing for delusion reinforcement (An Echo Chamber of One). After the April 2025 GPT-4o rollback, OpenAI made behavior issues launch-blocking (OpenAI).

Honesty and lying

Knowing the truth is not the same as telling it: bigger models know more facts but are no more honest when pressured to lie (MASK). Lie detectors that work on familiar lies often fail on new ones, especially lies the conversation alone can't reveal (Anthropic; Liars' Bench).

Tools a small team can use

  • Inspect Evals, now maintained by the nonprofit Generality Labs, includes a sycophancy eval and PersistBench.
  • Petri, Anthropic's auditing agent, moved to the nonprofit Meridian Labs in May 2026; version 3.0 tests models with their real system prompts to reduce test detection.
  • Bloom generates behavioral evals automatically.
  • Transluce's Docent helps analyze large sets of agent transcripts.

The field: people, money and neglected areas

The field is small and short of people more than money. About 1,100 people worked full-time on AI safety in 2025, roughly 600 technical and 500 governance (McAleese). 80,000 Hours calls that an undercount and says "a few thousand" (80,000 Hours).

  • Money. Coefficient Giving committed $351 million to technical AI safety, security and fieldbuilding in 2025. It expects about $1 billion in 2026 across all its catastrophic-risk work, including biosecurity (Manifund). Big tech's data-center spending neared $500 billion in 2025 (Epoch AI).
  • Talent. Fellowships now train about 2,000–2,500 people a year. Hiring managers say the scarcest people are senior researchers who can mentor, and generalists who can manage and found organizations (MATS; Topaz et al.).
  • What funders want. Funder and lab agendas converge on evidence about real-world model behavior, independent assessment of AI companies, security and verification (including training-data supply chains), new research institutions and talent (Tailwind; Anthropic's research directions). Robotics appears on none of the agendas the survey could read.
