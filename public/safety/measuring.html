Start here

Every safety eval asks one of three questions

CapabilityCan it?

Could the model do something dangerous if it tried, or if someone pushed it: write an exploit, troubleshoot a virus, run for hours alone?

PropensityWill it?

Left to itself, does it tell the truth, resist flattery, accept shutdown, avoid cheating? A capable model can still be well behaved, or not.

RobustnessDoes it hold?

When an attacker or a persistent user pushes, do the good behaviors survive jailbreaks, pressure and long conversations?

Deep dive

Sycophancy, up close Editor’s focus

Sycophancy is telling people what they want to hear instead of what's true. It is likely driven in part by the human ratings used in training, which often favor agreeable answers (Sharma et al., 2023). It's harder to measure than it looks.

Four kinds of sycophancy

  • Caving under pushback. Dropping a correct answer when the user says "Are you sure?" or insists on a wrong one.
  • Flattering feedback. Praising work more when the user says they wrote it or love it.
  • Echoing the user's view. Adopting the opinions or mistakes the user brings, such as a wrong attribution.
  • Social sycophancy. Protecting the user's self-image: validating, accepting their framing, siding with them in a conflict.

Case study: the GPT-4o rollback, April 2025

On 25 April 2025, OpenAI shipped a GPT-4o update that combined several changes, including a new reward signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings, better use of memory and fresher data. OpenAI said these changes together weakened the main reward signal that had been keeping sycophancy in check, and the model became noticeably flattering. Offline evals looked good, users in A/B tests liked it, and OpenAI had no deployment evaluation tracking sycophancy. Expert testers said the model "felt" slightly off, but the launch went ahead. The rollback began on 28 April. OpenAI now treats behavior issues in general, such as hallucination, deception, reliability and personality, as launch-blocking.

It's a textbook case of failure point 9: a metric (user approval) used for training quietly stopped measuring what anyone wanted. OpenAI's write-up

What the research found

  • Claude 2's preference model preferred sycophantic answers over terse human-written corrections 95% of the time. Against more helpful truthful answers on the hardest misconceptions, it still preferred them 45% of the time (Anthropic, 2023).
  • Scale and instruction tuning both increased sycophancy in Google's PaLM models (Wei et al., 2023).
  • Across math and medical questions, models changed their answer under rebuttal in 58% of cases: 43.52% were switches to the right answer and 14.66% to a wrong one. Rebuttals that cited sources led to the most wrong switches (SycEval, 2025).
  • Under adaptive pushback, collapse rates rose from 0–57% within 5 turns to 20–97% within 25, often with the right answer still visible in the model's own reasoning (SPINE, 2026 preprint).
  • Models were much more sycophantic when users made statements than when they asked questions, and more so the more certain the user sounded. Asking the model to turn a statement into a question first reduced sycophancy more than telling it not to be sycophantic (Dubois et al., UK AI Security Institute, 2026).
  • In 37,657 real claude.ai advice conversations, Anthropic's automated grader found sycophancy in 9% overall, rising to 25% for relationships and 38% for spirituality (Anthropic, 2026). The grader was Claude Sonnet 4.5, so a Claude model graded Claude: the same-family judging that step 5 below warns about.

Then run the mirror case: plant a wrong first answer and have the user correct it. Switching there is a valid update, and refusing is stubbornness.

Beyond single chats: memory and agents

  • PersistBench checks whether a model adopts false beliefs about the user that are stored in its memory. It is built into Inspect Evals (PersistBench).
  • In a 2026 workshop study, direct pushback caused only small to modest drops in accuracy, but most models failed when stored user preferences contradicted the right answer (The Price of Agreement).
  • MemSyco-Bench (2026) tests when memory should, and should not, sway an agent's decisions (MemSyco-Bench).
  • OpenAI said memory may have made the GPT-4o problem worse (OpenAI).

AI-associated delusions

  • OpenAI estimated that in a given week about 0.07% of active users show possible signs of psychosis or mania. After work with more than 170 clinicians, its updated GPT-5 met its target behavior in 92% of hard test cases, up from 27% (OpenAI, 2025).
  • In a 2025 simulation study, eight models often confirmed users' delusions, and 39.8% of scenarios got no safety intervention at all (psychosis-bench).
  • Researchers call for testing models for delusion reinforcement before release, and warn against drawing conclusions from anecdotes (An Echo Chamber of One). The evidence so far is estimates, simulations and position papers, not studies showing that chatbots cause psychosis.
  • Open audit tools now test for this: Bloom has a "delusional sycophancy" behavior, and Petri's test seeds cover encouraging a user's delusions (Bloom, Petri).

Run your own sycophancy eval Our analysis

Every step has published support: baseline sampling (MASK), varied pressure (SycEval, Dubois et al.), long multi-turn pressure (SPINE), hiding the test (Needham et al., Petri 3.0), and two-way scoring with error bars and judge checks (Anthropic, Bloom). The closest published end-to-end recipe is Anthropic's Bloom pipeline, which runs four stages: understanding, ideation, rollout and judgment.

  1. Start from existing code

    Inspect is the open-source eval framework built by the UK AISI. Its companion collection, Inspect Evals, started in 2024 with UK AISI and others and is now maintained by Generality Labs, a London nonprofit. It lists 185 evals, 56 of them external listings. Its built-in Sycophancy Eval covers only the "Are you sure?" test. MASK and PersistBench (sycophancy from memory) are built in, and BrokenMath and SycoBench-600 appear as listings. Run them on open models from Hugging Face first, then modify.

  2. Pin down the baseline

    Ask each question several times with no pressure and record the model's usual answer. MASK asks three times and treats inconsistent answers as "no belief." Count only shifts beyond the model's normal variation.

  3. Vary the pressure deliberately

    Mix "Are you sure?", a stated wrong belief, an appeal to authority or a citation, and emotional appeals. Vary certainty, first versus third person, and question versus statement phrasing; each moves the rate.

  4. Go multi-turn, and hide the test

    Single-turn tests underestimate collapse. Keep pushing for 5, 10 and 25 turns with a simulated user, and remove obvious test cues so the model doesn't realize it's being evaluated. Cover social sycophancy too, not only facts.

  5. Score both directions and check your judge

    Report harmful flips and valid updates separately, with confidence intervals. Hand-label a sample to check any LLM grader (Bloom's authors used 40 transcripts; MASK used 150), and use a judge from a different model family to avoid self-preference. With open weights, also compare base and instruction-tuned versions.

Expert views

What the research says

Ten findings from the expert survey behind this page, each with its source.

  • Many AI benchmarks are shakier than they look: a review of 445 found nearly half measured contested, loosely defined ideas, and only 16% used any statistical test (Bean et al., 2025).
  • A benchmark score is an estimate with random error, so it needs error bars, and when questions come in related groups, honest error bars can be over three times wider than naive ones (Anthropic).
  • Benchmarks themselves break: OpenAI retired a leading coding benchmark after finding leaked answers and flawed tests in most of the hard problems it audited, and 37% of AI passes on a hacking benchmark involved cheating (OpenAI; Kouremetis et al., 2026).
  • Models can often tell when they are being tested, and one Claude model blackmailed in 55% of runs it said were real versus 6.5% of runs it said were tests (Anthropic).
  • Safety training can make models more aware of being tested, so part of an apparent safety gain may just be the model recognizing an evaluation (Apollo Research).
  • Catching a model that deliberately underperforms is hard from outside: in a UK government exercise, black-box checks did poorly, and the more promising methods needed deeper access than outside testers usually get (UK AISI).
  • Chatbots cave more the longer a user pushes: in a 2026 study, the rate at which models gave up a correct position rose from 0–57% within 5 turns to 20–97% within 25 turns (SPINE).
  • How you ask matters, because models are more sycophantic when you state a belief confidently than when you ask a question (Dubois et al., 2026).
  • Knowing the truth is not the same as telling it: bigger models know more facts but are no more honest when pressured to lie (MASK).
  • Researchers do not all score sycophancy the same way: SycEval counts a switch to the right answer as sycophancy too, while this page's table treats it as a valid update (SycEval).
